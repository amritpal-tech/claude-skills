<!-- post:amritpal format:single-image ask:sharp-question type:news -->
"AI doesn't cite your own website" is only half true.

Three citation studies came out in early November, and they split on exactly this.

Lightsite: company product and service pages were the biggest source group, at 41% of ChatGPT citations and 45% of Perplexity citations from July to November.

Paradigm Media Networks, nearly 370,000 citations: the engines lean on video, community threads, reference sites, news and "best of" lists ahead of the brand's own site. YouTube was the most-cited site in AI Overviews.

Hasmord, 60 B2B buying questions asked three times each: media and publishers showed up far less in B2B answers than in shopping answers.

So which is it?

Here's the actual mechanism, as I read it. The question set decides the result.

Ask what a product does, costs or connects to, and the vendor's own page is the best source. Ask what's best in a category, and the engines go looking for third parties.

Three studies, three question sets, three results.

Here's how I'd use that for a B2B site:

1. Split your tracking prompts into two buckets. Buying questions: pricing, features, integrations, comparisons. Category questions: best tools for X, how to choose.
2. For buying questions, make your own pages extractable. One question per section, the answer in the first two lines, real numbers.
3. For category questions, earn the sources the engines already use: reviews, YouTube, community threads, the lists that get cited.
4. Report the two buckets separately. A blended citation rate hides both problems.

One honest disqualifier: different methods, different samples, no shared definitions. Read them as direction.

Ranking earns the crawl. The question decides who gets cited.

Which bucket are you losing right now, buying questions or category questions?

The reps who spent most on AI sold less.

That's from Stephanie Jenkins, who leads sales at PandaDoc. She shared the numbers on LinkedIn.

At the start of the year, PandaDoc gave all of its 50-plus reps unlimited access to Claude and encouraged them to use it.

Then she compared each rep's AI spend with their quota attainment.

The correlation ran negative. Her figure: for every $100 of AI spend, sales performance dropped about 2%.

Her read, as reported: reps were building tools that already existed and spending time on work that didn't move deals, instead of talking to customers.

Her fix: centralise it. Ops and enablement build the AI tools, like pipeline summaries, demo prep and renewal next steps. Reps use them.

Here's the honest part.

It's one company and a correlation. Commenters pointed out that tenure could explain some of it. Nothing here shows Claude caused lower sales.

But the pattern is easy to believe. Give every rep a blank AI canvas and some of them become part-time builders.

The realist read for anyone budgeting for this:

1. Fund a few builders before you fund every seat. Ops builds, reps use.
2. Measure outcomes per rep alongside spend per rep.
3. Protect selling time. If AI hours come out of customer hours, the math breaks.

Automate the typing. Never automate the judgment. And keep your closers out of the tool-building business.

Repost if your team handed out AI licences and called it a strategy.

<!-- post:pankaj format:single-image ask:repost-if type:news -->
Your HubSpot code actions freeze on November 17.

HubSpot's developer changelog says custom code actions in workflows can now run on Node.js 24.

The older runtimes are on their way out:

↳ November 3: no new custom code actions on Node.js 20.

↳ November 17: no more edits to existing Node.js 20 actions.

↳ February 1, 2027: serverless functions built in the design manager stop running.

Existing Node.js 16 and 20 actions keep running for now.

That's the trap. Nothing breaks next week, so nobody migrates. Then a property gets renamed, an API changes, and the fix needs an edit you can no longer make.

And moving to Node.js 24 takes real work. Callback-style handlers aren't supported, and the libraries your code can load have new versions.

Here's how I'd use the next seven days:

1. List every workflow with a custom code action, and the runtime each one uses.
2. Rank them by what breaks if they fail: routing, lead scoring, enrichment writes, deal stage changes.
3. Rewrite callback handlers as async functions, then retest against the new library versions.
4. Clone the workflow, run the Node.js 24 copy on test records, compare the outputs, then switch.
5. Put anything on design manager serverless functions on the list for February.

Here is the line that splits this room: code nobody can edit is a liability, even while it still runs.

Repost if someone on your team owns HubSpot workflows.
