<!-- post:amritpal format:single-image ask:sharp-question type:news -->
AI traffic grew 45%. It's still 0.36% of visits.

That's from SEOcrawl AI's study, published November 6. Visits from AI assistants grew about 45% year over year, and their share of total website traffic rose from 0.24% to 0.36%.

That's just over 1 visit for every 100 from Google.

Inside that sliver, ChatGPT sends 83%. Gemini 6%, Perplexity 5%, Claude 4%, Copilot 2%.

Two easy readings, and both are wrong.

"AI search is a rounding error." The study itself says AI is undercounted. ChatGPT app visits usually lose their referrer and land in direct. Google's AI answers are logged as organic.

"AI search is replacing Google." Not on these numbers.

Here's how I'd measure it for a B2B site:

1. Give AI assistants their own channel in analytics: chatgpt.com, perplexity.ai, gemini.google.com, claude.ai, copilot.microsoft.com.
2. Watch direct traffic to deep pages. Nobody types a nine-word URL, so a rise there can be an app referral that lost its tag.
3. Ask on the form. A "how did you hear about us" field with an AI option catches what analytics can't.
4. Compare conversion rates as well as volume. A small channel that converts well deserves budget.

One honest limit: this is one study, with a sample reported as 3,000 sites in one copy and 16,000 in another, and a referral-based method that misses part of the story.

Small, growing, undercounted. Measure all three.

What share of your "direct" traffic do you think is really ChatGPT?

Your team's LinkedIn posts are now AI search results.

A separate Semrush study of 325,000 prompts put LinkedIn second overall, behind Reddit, appearing in about 11% of AI responses.

Here's the leader read.

When a buyer asks an AI assistant about your category, the answer is partly built from what people wrote on LinkedIn. Possibly your people.

That moves team posting from a brand nice-to-have into how buyers find you.

Three things I'd change:

1. Treat team posts as a channel. Give them a calendar, an owner and a review step, like anything else that touches buyers.
2. Write for the question. Posts that answer one specific buyer question in plain words are the ones worth citing.
3. Measure it. Run your top buyer questions through the assistants monthly and note whose posts get cited.

The company page is no longer the only page that speaks for you.

Repost if your team posts on LinkedIn and nobody owns it as a channel.

### Post
<!-- post:pankaj format:single-image ask:sharp-question type:curation -->
Four of your GTM vendors now ship their own agents.

Since late September, Gong announced an Agent Builder, Apollo an AI GTM System, ZoomInfo launched Agent Teams, and Clay showed a Global Agent at its Sculpt event.

Each one is reasonable on its own. Together, they're four agents that could touch the same account, the same contact, the same CRM record.

That's the part no vendor demo covers.

Here's how I'd keep them from stepping on each other:

1. One system of record. Every agent writes to the CRM through one path, so there's one history.
2. One owner per action. Decide which agent may enrich, which may sequence, which may change a stage. Overlap is where duplicates come from.
3. A shared do-not-touch list. Suppressions, open opportunities, customers in renewal. Every agent reads it before acting.
4. Agent IDs on every write. When a record changes, you should know which vendor's agent changed it.

Honest limit: launch details vary by source, and some of these are partly available or in beta. Check each vendor's current docs before you plan around them.

Four smart agents with no traffic rules is a pile-up waiting to happen.

How many vendors in your stack can now write to your CRM without a human?
